# Great Scott Tree Care Employee Handbook — California Legal-Risk Review

**Review date:** July 11, 2026  
**Document reviewed:** “GSTC Employee Handbook (Revised 2026)” (draft for executive review)  
**Scope:** California and federal employment-law issue spotting. This is an attorney worklist, not a substitute for advice based on GSTC’s worksites, workforce, CBAs, benefit plans, payroll practices, or local ordinances.

## Executive priority

Do not issue the handbook in its present form. It contains materially obsolete and contradictory leave provisions, unlawful CFRA eligibility and spouse-aggregation language, risky sick-leave certification and rehire rules, an overbroad investigation-confidentiality command, questionable payroll deductions/repayment provisions, and drug-testing language inconsistent with California’s cannabis protections. The safety program also uses obsolete terminology and is not a reliable substitute for the tree-work-specific written programs required by Cal/OSHA.

The handbook appears to be several generations of policies pasted together. Duplicate policies conflict with one another, and several expired statutes remain presented as operative. A California employment attorney should supervise a clean consolidation—not isolated edits—and payroll/operations should be audited against the corrected policies.

## HIGH severity issues

### 1. CFRA eligibility test unlawfully retains the former 50-employees/75-miles requirement

- **Location:** Leaves of Absence → California Family Rights Act (CFRA) Leave, “Eligibility” (source lines 1837–1852).
- **Problem:** The policy says: **“You must work within 75 miles of a Company worksite that employs 50 or more employees.”** California’s current CFRA applies to employers with five or more employees and has no 50-within-75-miles employee eligibility test. GSTC’s own history says it has over 100 employees.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Denial of CFRA leave, reinstatement, or benefits based on this test can support claims under Gov. Code §§ 12945.2 and 12945.6 and 2 C.C.R. § 11087 et seq., including compensatory damages, attorney fees, and individual liability for retaliation in appropriate cases.
- **Recommendation:** Delete the quoted test. State the current employer-coverage rule and retain only employee service requirements (12 months and 1,250 hours), subject to current law. Audit every leave denial since January 1, 2021 that relied on worksite headcount.

### 2. CFRA family definition omits/designates family members incorrectly

- **Location:** CFRA Leave, qualifying reasons (source lines 1839–1845).
- **Problem:** It covers **“child, parent, sibling, grandparent, grandchildren, and spouse”** but omits a **designated person** and uses the plural “grandchildren.” Current CFRA includes child, parent, grandparent, grandchild, sibling, spouse, domestic partner, and a designated person (subject to the statutory designation rules).
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Employees could be denied protected family-care leave, producing interference and retaliation exposure under CFRA/FEHA.
- **Recommendation:** Replace the list with the complete current statutory definition, expressly include registered domestic partner and designated person, explain the one-designated-person-per-12-month limitation if GSTC elects to require advance designation, and cross-check the paid-sick-leave definition separately.

### 3. CFRA spouse aggregation is obsolete and unlawful

- **Location:** CFRA Leave → “Spouse Aggregation” (source lines 1863–1868); New Parent Leave (source lines 1978–2009).
- **Problem:** The handbook states: **“If you and your spouse are both employed by GSTC, the combined total number of weeks … will be limited to 12 weeks per leave year.”** It repeats that both parents get a combined maximum of 12 weeks. Current CFRA gives each eligible employee up to 12 weeks; the former aggregation rule was removed.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Direct under-allocation of protected bonding/family leave and reinstatement rights; classwide exposure if applied systematically.
- **Recommendation:** Delete all CFRA/New Parent aggregation language. State that each eligible employee receives the full CFRA entitlement. Keep any FMLA aggregation rule confined to circumstances federal law currently permits, and clearly separate it from CFRA.

### 4. “New Parent Leave” uses repealed 20-employee/75-mile law and conflicts with CFRA

- **Location:** Leaves → New Parent Leave (source lines 1978–2010).
- **Problem:** Eligibility requires **“at least 20 employees within a 75-mile radius,”** reflecting the repealed New Parent Leave Act. It duplicates and conflicts with CFRA and repeats unlawful spouse aggregation.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Staff may administer an obsolete eligibility rule and deny current CFRA rights.
- **Recommendation:** Delete this section. Address bonding only in a single current CFRA/FMLA policy, explaining concurrency where lawful and the separate availability of CFRA bonding after pregnancy disability/FMLA pregnancy leave.

### 5. FMLA bonding provisions contradict each other and unlawfully restrict intermittent leave under CFRA

- **Location:** FMLA Policy → Leave Increments → Parental Leave (source lines 1770–1785).
- **Problem:** One paragraph says bonding leave **“must be taken in a single block and cannot be taken on an intermittent or reduced schedule basis.”** The next says it **“can be taken intermittently but is limited to a maximum of 2 separate block periods,”** with two-week minimums. Under CFRA, an employee may take bonding leave in increments of two weeks, but must be allowed two occasions of shorter duration; there is no “maximum of 2 block periods.” Federal FMLA intermittent bonding may depend on employer agreement, making a combined policy especially misleading.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Denial or discouragement of CFRA bonding leave and inconsistent administration.
- **Recommendation:** Separate FMLA and CFRA rules. Accurately state CFRA’s two-week default increment and the two shorter-duration occasions, and state the distinct federal rule. Remove both contradictory passages.

### 6. Paid sick leave documentation, reasons, family definition, and rehire rules are defective

- **Location:** Paid Sick Leave (source lines 2020–2048); Final Pay (source lines 717–754).
- **Problem:** The policy says employees **“may be asked to provide a certification of illness”** without limiting documentation requests; its reasons/family list is outdated (including omission of designated person and current qualifying-act-of-violence uses); and it promises reinstatement only if rehired **“within the same calendar year,”** while California generally requires reinstatement when rehired within one year. It also says unused frontloaded leave **“expires,”** language that should be distinguished from unlawful forfeiture of accrued leave and coordinated with local ordinances.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Labor Code §§ 245–249 protect paid sick leave and prohibit denial/retaliation. Overbroad documentation can interfere with leave; incorrect reinstatement creates measurable wage exposure and possible PAGA claims. Local ordinances may be more protective.
- **Recommendation:** Rewrite to current California and applicable local law. Limit reasonable documentation requests to circumstances permitted by law and not as a condition for ordinary foreseeable/unforeseeable use. Add all current purposes and family/designated-person definitions, correct rehire to one year, preserve the 40-hour/5-day statutory floor (or higher local rule), and define frontload/accrual/carryover cleanly.

### 7. Bereavement policy counts non-workdays and contains conflicting entitlements

- **Location:** Bereavement Leave (source lines 2049–2055).
- **Problem:** It first promises three paid days, then five days with three paid, and states: **“Any scheduled days off (including weekends, holidays and vacation) falling during the absence will be counted as both bereavement leave and scheduled days off.”** California’s five statutory days need not be consecutive but must be completed within three months; counting days the employee was not scheduled to work can unlawfully shrink leave. The family definition is also not aligned precisely with Gov. Code § 12945.7.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Denial of protected bereavement leave and retaliation/FEHA remedies.
- **Recommendation:** Replace both paragraphs with one policy providing at least five days per qualifying death, completed within three months, counting only workdays missed, with the current statutory family definition, confidentiality, permissible documentation, and nonretaliation. Clearly state which days GSTC voluntarily pays.

### 8. Investigation confidentiality mandate is overbroad

- **Location:** EEO/Harassment → Reporting and Investigations (source lines 220–238).
- **Problem:** **“Anyone involved in an investigation of harassment has an obligation to keep all information about the investigation confidential. … Failure … may result in disciplinary action.”** A blanket rule can chill employees from discussing workplace conditions, obtaining support, coordinating complaints, or participating in protected concerted activity. The employer also cannot promise complete confidentiality.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** NLRA § 7/§ 8(a)(1) exposure under the fact-specific standard governing investigative-confidentiality rules; FEHA retaliation/interference concerns; whistleblower chilling under Labor Code §§ 1102.5 and 232.5.
- **Recommendation:** Replace with a narrowly tailored request: GSTC will preserve privacy to the extent practicable; employees must not retaliate, destroy evidence, knowingly provide false information, or improperly disclose protected personal/medical data. Any case-specific confidentiality direction should be documented as necessary for legitimate investigative reasons and reviewed by counsel.

### 9. Drug policy allows action based on nonimpairing metabolites and overbroad off-duty conduct

- **Location:** Drug and Alcohol Policy; Cannabis; Reasonable Suspicion; Pre-Employment Screening (source lines 263–298, 444–452).
- **Problem:** It prohibits reporting with **“a measurable quantity of any alcohol, drug, intoxicant or narcotic in the blood or urine,”** allows a positive test to lead to termination, and requires applicants broadly to undergo drug testing. The cannabis section promises no adverse action for off-duty use, but the measurable-quantity/positive-test language conflicts with it. California AB 2188/SB 700 protections generally prohibit discrimination based on off-duty cannabis use or tests detecting nonpsychoactive metabolites, subject to statutory exceptions (including certain building/construction trades and federal requirements).
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** FEHA/Gov. Code § 12954 claims, privacy claims, disability-discrimination issues concerning lawful medication, and inconsistent discipline. The “sole opinion” of GSTC clause undermines objective assessment.
- **Recommendation:** Have counsel map each safety-sensitive job and statutory exception. Test only through a lawful, validated protocol; distinguish present impairment from historical use; exclude nonpsychoactive-metabolite testing unless a documented exception applies; use trained-observer reasonable-suspicion forms and confirmatory testing/MRO review; protect lawful medications and accommodations; define refusal and chain of custody.

### 10. Mandatory reporting of any drug/alcohol-related charge is overbroad

- **Location:** Drug and Alcohol Policy (source lines 268–276); Criminal Activity/Arrests (source lines 872–875).
- **Problem:** An employee must report any **“charged with, or convicted”** violation that **“in any way involves”** alcohol or drugs by the next workday; off-property criminal involvement may result in termination. California sharply limits use of arrests not resulting in conviction and requires job-related, individualized consideration for conviction history.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Labor Code § 432.7, FEHA/fair-chance regulations, local fair-chance rules, privacy, and disparate-impact exposure.
- **Recommendation:** Delete mandatory reporting of mere charges/arrests except where a narrowly applicable law requires it. Create a counsel-reviewed conviction policy tied to job duties, timing, nature/gravity, required notices, and individual assessment; preserve rules for loss of a required license and work-related conduct.

### 11. Final-wage deductions and employee repayment provisions are unlawful or dangerously broad

- **Location:** Uniform Policy; credit cards; company devices/technology; New Parent Leave benefits; certification tuition (source lines 994–1002, 1036–1040, 1133–1278, 1470–1482, 1994–2003).
- **Problem:** Examples include **“responsible for the cost of unreturned uniforms at the time of termination,”** **“responsible for any lost or damaged equipment,”** reimbursement within five days for card purchases, 50% tuition repayment based on not giving one’s “best,” and recovering premiums **“by deducting the amount from your final wages.”** California generally bars unilateral deductions for ordinary losses, breakage, cash shortages, and debts absent narrow legal conditions and proper authorization; final wages cannot be self-help for disputed debts.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Labor Code §§ 221–224, 400–410, 2802, final-pay waiting-time penalties (§§ 201–203), wage-statement/PAGA claims, and DLSE enforcement.
- **Recommendation:** Remove all authorization for final-pay deductions except deductions expressly lawful. State that GSTC may pursue separate lawful recovery for willful/dishonest loss after individualized review. Employer-required uniforms/tools must be provided and maintained as required. Counsel should draft any tuition agreement prospectively, with objective terms, no minimum-wage impact, no penalty, and no deduction from wages.

### 12. Reimbursement policy improperly makes statutory reimbursement contingent on approval/deadlines

- **Location:** Business Expenses (source lines 931–961); Travel Expenses (969–993); Continuing Education (1483–1491).
- **Problem:** “Reimbursable” expenses include personal phones/tools only as **“approved”** and requests are **“subject to review and approval”** and deadlines. Travel requires prior approval. Labor Code § 2802 requires indemnification of necessary expenditures incurred in direct consequence of duties; failure to obtain preapproval may justify discipline but does not necessarily eliminate reimbursement.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Class/PAGA exposure for phones, mileage, tools, travel, remote work, and delayed reimbursement; interest and attorney fees under § 2802.
- **Recommendation:** State clearly that all necessary and reasonable business expenses will be reimbursed even if authorization procedures were not followed, while reserving discipline for bypassing procedures. Define submission channel, reasonable time, mileage rate, cell-phone stipend/reasonable percentage, tool rules under the wage order, and escalation for disputed claims. Audit actual field expenses.

### 13. “Holiday & vacation pay allowance” may conceal vested vacation and regular-rate errors

- **Location:** Benefits → Holiday & Vacation Pay Allowance—Nonexempt Field Employees (source lines 1330–1340).
- **Problem:** The policy combines “holiday & vacation” into a per-hour allowance paid each payroll and says no pay is received when vacation occurs. Labeling alone does not determine whether compensation is vested vacation under Labor Code § 227.3. If the allowance is promised for hours worked, it may also need inclusion in the overtime regular rate depending on structure.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Vacation forfeiture/final-pay, overtime regular-rate, wage-statement, and PAGA exposure across the field workforce.
- **Recommendation:** Counsel and payroll must analyze the plan documents and actual pay codes. Either create a compliant vacation accrual/use/payout policy or clearly define a lawful wage supplement without representing it as vacation. Recalculate regular rate and final pay historically if required.

### 14. Emergency incentive may be misclassified as outside wages and excluded from regular rate

- **Location:** Meal and Rest Periods → Emergency Incentive (source lines 627–633).
- **Problem:** It says the incentive **“is separate from wages and overtime compensation required by law”** and discretionary. A label does not make a promised or formula-based incentive excludable from the regular rate.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** FLSA and California overtime underpayment, derivative wage statements/final pay, and PAGA.
- **Recommendation:** Define eligibility and calculation. Payroll must include nondiscretionary incentives in the regular rate and perform retroactive true-ups where needed; reserve “discretionary” only where both payment and amount truly remain at employer discretion until near payment.

### 15. Work-time rules invite uncompensated pre/post-shift work and improperly punish “off-the-clock” work

- **Location:** Recording Time; Work Schedule; Standards of Conduct (source lines 555–581, 827–859).
- **Problem:** Employees should clock in no more than five minutes early/out no more than five minutes late; must be dressed and ready at shift start; and **“working ‘off the clock’”** is defined as falsifying records and grounds for termination. Field crews may have compensable yard reporting, loading, PPE, vehicle inspection, travel, phone/app, training, and post-shift duties. Punishing the worker for unrecorded time can chill reporting even though GSTC must pay known/suffered work.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** California wage-order and Labor Code §§ 510, 1194, 1174 exposure; FLSA; waiting-time, wage-statement, class and PAGA claims.
- **Recommendation:** Say unequivocally that all work must be recorded and will be paid, even if unauthorized; discipline may address procedure violations but never reduce pay or deter correction. Remove “off the clock” from “falsification.” Prohibit supervisors from allowing pre/post-shift tasks and audit travel, loading, donning, phones, and rounding practices.

### 16. Meal/rest policy is incomplete for field operations and uses ambiguous premium trigger

- **Location:** Meal and Rest Periods (source lines 598–626).
- **Problem:** The basic timing is mostly correct, but **“if an employee is unable to take”** a compliant period is narrower/less clear than the employer’s premium obligation when it fails to provide a compliant period. It lacks on-duty meal requirements/revocation language, does not explain separate premiums where both meal and rest violations occur in one day, and “remain on premises/job sites” must not impose duties or unreasonable control. Mobile crews create practical first/second meal and rest-break risks.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Wage Order 4/5/16 applicability must be determined; Labor Code §§ 226.7 and 512; regular-rate premium rules after *Ferra*; class/PAGA exposure.
- **Recommendation:** Identify the correct wage order(s). State employees are relieved of all duty and free to leave for meals; define lawful written, revocable on-duty meal agreements only where the nature of work prevents relief; state premiums are paid automatically upon Company knowledge, at the regular rate, with separate meal/rest categories as required. Implement dispatch/timekeeping exception prompts and audit crew schedules.

### 17. Attendance/job-abandonment rules can automatically terminate protected absences

- **Location:** Attendance, Job Abandonment, Personal Leave, FMLA/CFRA/PDL (source lines 518–552, 1495–1549, 1694–1909, 2063–2155).
- **Problem:** “Excessive” absence is determined in GSTC’s **“sole discretion”;** two **“(3)”** or three days may be voluntary resignation; and failure to return/request extension is automatically deemed resignation. These rules do not adequately require screening for sick leave, CFRA/FMLA, PDL, disability accommodation, workers’ compensation, qualifying violence, or other protected reasons. “Voluntary resignation” does not erase an unlawful termination.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** FEHA/ADA failure to accommodate and retaliation; leave interference; paid-sick-leave retaliation; workers’ compensation discrimination under Labor Code § 132a.
- **Recommendation:** Add an express protected-absence carveout and mandatory HR/legal review before discipline or abandonment. Require outreach and interactive-process analysis; correct “two (3).” Do not automatically terminate at leave expiration where additional finite accommodation may be reasonable.

### 18. PDL certification/second-opinion and denial language is inconsistent with California rules

- **Location:** Pregnancy Disability Leave (source lines 2063–2155).
- **Problem:** The policy says paperwork failures **“will result in the delay or denial of leave”** and permits GSTC to demand **“a second and third certification from another health care provider at Company expense,”** apparently copied from FMLA. California PDL certification procedures do not generally authorize employer-selected second/third opinions in that manner. It also requires a return release categorically and threatens automatic termination.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Gov. Code § 12945 and 2 C.C.R. §§ 11035–11051 interference, failure to accommodate/transfer, and retaliation.
- **Recommendation:** Replace with a California-specific PDL policy using the CRD regulatory certification content/timing, reasonable opportunity to cure deficiencies, legally permissible fitness-for-duty rules applied uniformly, and an interactive-process bridge to additional FEHA accommodation.

### 19. Organ-donor leave requires too much accrued leave

- **Location:** Bone Marrow and Organ Donor Leave (source lines 1824–1836).
- **Problem:** **“Before taking paid leave … you must first use two weeks of accrued sick or vacation time when donating an organ, or five days … when donating bone marrow.”** California permits an employer to require up to two weeks of earned unused sick/vacation for organ donation, but only up to five days for bone marrow; however, current organ leave also includes up to 30 additional unpaid business days, and wording must not treat protected paid leave as merely employee-funded. Coordination and entitlement should be checked carefully.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Labor Code §§ 1508–1513 denial/retaliation and reinstatement exposure.
- **Recommendation:** Counsel should rewrite to track the exact current entitlement and permissible exhaustion, clarify the employer-paid component after permitted exhaustion, additional unpaid organ leave, nonconcurrency with FMLA/CFRA as applicable, benefits, reinstatement, and retaliation.

### 20. Safety program is obsolete and insufficiently tree-work-specific

- **Location:** Safety and Loss Prevention; IIPP; Hazard Communication (source lines 2202–2524).
- **Problem:** The handbook refers to **“OSHA 200”** and **“MSDS”** rather than current OSHA 300 records and Safety Data Sheets/GHS. PPE examples include **“bump caps”** despite severe struck-by hazards. The generic program does not state critical tree-work controls for line-clearance/electrical hazards, minimum approach distances, qualified tree workers, aerial lifts, climbing/arborist equipment, chain saws, chippers, traffic control, drop zones, rescue, hearing/respiratory protection, pesticide rules, or wildfire smoke. “Only employees with a valid first aid care card can perform first aid” could deter emergency aid. Annual formal training alone is not enough.
- **Legal risk — HIGH:** Cal/OSHA citations and serious/willful penalties; tort/Workers’ Compensation consequences after catastrophic injury; Title 8 rules including §§ 3203, 3395, 3420 et seq. (tree work), 3648, 2940 et seq., 5144, 5194, 5141.1, and applicable logging/chipper/traffic rules.
- **Recommendation:** Do not rely on the handbook as the IIPP. Engage qualified California tree-care safety counsel/consultant to audit the written IIPP, Heat Illness Prevention Plan, wildfire-smoke plan, hazard communication, PPE assessment, electrical/line-clearance qualifications, aerial devices, climbing/chipper/chain-saw procedures, rescue plans, first aid, traffic control, and training records. Replace obsolete terms and name responsible persons and access methods in the actual programs.

## MEDIUM severity issues

### 21. EEO protected-class lists are inconsistent and omit current California wording

- **Location:** EEO Statement and Policy Against Harassment (source lines 168–244).
- **Problem:** Different paragraphs use different lists. The opening EEO list omits reproductive health decisionmaking (later included), registered domestic partnership, and immigration status/language protections; it says “domestic violence victim status” rather than current broader qualifying-act-of-violence protections. The text reads **“CRD DFEH”** and **“California Department of Fair Employment and Housing,”** an obsolete agency name.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** An incomplete policy may discourage complaints and weaken GSTC’s preventive-defense evidence under FEHA; inconsistent lists confuse managers.
- **Recommendation:** Use one current protected-class formulation throughout, including association/perceived status and all applicable local categories. Use “California Civil Rights Department (CRD)” only and current contact/filing information. State the policy applies to all terms, contractors/interns/volunteers where required.

### 22. Harassment definition incorrectly suggests hostile-environment conduct must “unreasonably interfere”

- **Location:** EEO/Harassment → Sexual Harassment (source lines 195–216).
- **Problem:** Hostile environment is described as conduct that **“unreasonably interferes with an employee’s work performance and/or creates”** an offensive environment. FEHA does not require lost productivity; actionable harassment depends on the totality, and even one incident can suffice if sufficiently severe. California also requires distribution of a compliant written prevention policy with specified elements.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** Managers may apply too high a threshold and fail to correct conduct before liability attaches.
- **Recommendation:** Use CRD-compliant language: conduct need not cause economic injury or impair productivity; a single severe incident may create a hostile environment; prohibited conduct may violate Company policy before it is unlawful. Confirm complaint routes include someone other than the direct supervisor and CRD/EEOC contact details.

### 23. Disability policy uses ADA-centric language and routes requests only through supervisor

- **Location:** Disability Accommodation (source lines 245–262); Personal Appearance; leave return rules.
- **Problem:** **“Otherwise qualified”** and “essential functions” are used without clearly reflecting broader FEHA coverage, and employees are told it is their responsibility to notify a supervisor. FEHA’s interactive-process duty can be triggered by employer knowledge; finite leave/reassignment may be required. Multiple leave sections automatically terminate employees rather than assess accommodation.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM/HIGH depending application:** FEHA/ADA interactive-process and reasonable-accommodation claims.
- **Recommendation:** State that requests may be oral, use no special words, and may be made to HR or management; GSTC will initiate the process when it knows of a possible need. Add reassignment and leave; prohibit automatic termination; keep medical inquiries narrowly job-related and confidential.

### 24. Religious accommodation standard is vague after *Groff*

- **Location:** Religious Accommodation (source lines 311–317).
- **Problem:** It lists cost and burdens but does not state the controlling federal substantial-increased-cost standard or California FEHA’s separate undue-hardship framework. **“At no time will GSTC question the validity”** is overabsolute; limited inquiry into sincerity may be lawful where objectively justified.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** Title VII and FEHA accommodation claims or inconsistent decisions.
- **Recommendation:** Adopt a good-faith interactive process, evaluate each request under both applicable standards, avoid burdening coworkers merely because of hostility, and allow only narrowly tailored sincerity inquiry where counsel approves.

### 25. Victim-leave provisions are outdated, duplicated, and narrower than current law

- **Location:** Accommodations for Victims (source lines 318–363); Crime Victim Leave; Leave for Victims (source lines 1910–1963); Paid Sick Leave.
- **Problem:** Three overlapping policies focus on domestic violence, sexual assault, or stalking and older documentation lists. California’s protections now use broader “qualifying act of violence” concepts and protect certain family members; employer coverage and entitlements vary. Duplicate rules invite denial.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM/HIGH:** Labor Code §§ 230 and 230.1/current successor provisions as amended, FEHA-related retaliation, paid-sick-leave violations.
- **Recommendation:** Replace with one current, counsel-verified victim/family-of-victim leave and accommodation policy stating all covered reasons, family/designated persons, notice/documentation alternatives, confidentiality, safety accommodations, paid-leave interaction, and anti-retaliation.

### 26. Personnel-file policy does not specify all statutory mechanics

- **Location:** Access to Personnel and Medical Records Files (source lines 906–914).
- **Problem:** It correctly mentions 30 days and payroll records within 21 days, but does not explain written-request mechanics, representative rights, former-employee access/location, copying cost limits, retention, or that certain investigation/reference materials may be excluded. “All employees” may imply current employees only.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** Labor Code §§ 1198.5 and 226 enforcement and penalties.
- **Recommendation:** Add current/former employee and authorized-representative procedures, statutory response periods and permitted extensions, inspection/copy methods, reasonable copying costs, exclusions, and retention obligations.

### 27. Monitoring/inspection rules are overbroad, especially personal containers and communications

- **Location:** Use of Company Technology; Workplace Privacy and Right to Inspect; Personal Cell Phone (source lines 1041–1053, 1158–1279, 1295–1301).
- **Problem:** GSTC claims it may monitor/intercept **“telephone conversation[s]”** without prior notice/consent and inspect **“any package or other container”** an employee possesses. California constitutional/common-law privacy and Penal Code wiretap/eavesdropping rules require context, notice/consent, and proportionality. A blanket personal-device recording ban can also interfere with protected evidence gathering and concerted activity.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM/HIGH:** Penal Code §§ 631–632, constitutional privacy, NLRA, whistleblower/FEHA evidence issues.
- **Recommendation:** Limit monitoring to lawful business systems and purposes with clear advance notice and consent; exclude privileged/legal and protected communications; do not intercept calls without legally sufficient consent. Require reasonable, work-related grounds and calibrated methods for personal-container searches; add protected-activity carveouts to recording rules.

### 28. Social media, confidentiality, conduct, and solicitation rules remain vulnerable to protected-activity challenges

- **Location:** Standards of Conduct; No Solicitation/Distribution; Social Media; Confidentiality; Third Party Disclosures (source lines 827–859, 1005–1015, 1061–1132).
- **Problem:** Rules prohibit “disrespect for management,” posts that “should not reflect upon or refer to GSTC,” and broadly require confidentiality/referral of outside contacts. A final NLRA savings clause helps but does not cure facially overbroad rules employees would reasonably read as restricting group complaints, union activity, media/government contact, or wage/safety discussion.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** NLRA § 7/§ 8(a)(1), Labor Code §§ 232, 232.5, 1102.5, and Government Code § 12964.5.
- **Recommendation:** Narrow misconduct to objectively abusive, discriminatory, threatening, knowingly false, or disloyal conduct unprotected by law. Expressly protect coworker organizing, wages/hours/safety discussions, lawful media/government communications, whistleblowing, and cooperation with agencies. Ensure solicitation rules treat union and nonunion activity consistently.

### 29. Invention-assignment policy lacks California statutory notice and is overbroad

- **Location:** Inventions (source lines 1119–1125).
- **Problem:** It claims any invention made during work hours or with facilities is work for hire and requires a waiver for anything that **“relates in any way”** to GSTC services. It does not include the mandatory Labor Code § 2870 limitation/notice associated with invention assignments, and “work for hire” is not a complete patent-ownership rule.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** Unenforceability and Labor Code §§ 2870–2872 disputes.
- **Recommendation:** Use a separate California invention-assignment agreement drafted by IP/employment counsel, attach the § 2870 notice, and limit assignment to protectable company-related inventions within the statute.

### 30. Physical examinations are described too broadly

- **Location:** Pre-Employment Screening and Promotion Physical Assessment (source lines 444–452, 810–826).
- **Problem:** **“All candidates selected for employment”** must undergo a physical exam, but the policy does not clearly say it occurs only after a real conditional offer and is required uniformly for all entering employees in the same job category. Promotion exams of current employees require job-relatedness and business necessity, not merely “pre-placement” framing.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** ADA 42 U.S.C. § 12112(d), FEHA medical-inquiry rules, privacy, and disparate treatment.
- **Recommendation:** Limit applicant medical exams to post-conditional-offer, same-job-category exams; keep medical data separate; conduct individualized accommodation review. For incumbents/promotions, require documented job-relatedness/business necessity and counsel review.

### 31. FMLA policy has notice, fitness, and premium-recovery overstatements

- **Location:** FMLA Policy (source lines 1694–1823).
- **Problem:** It says notice should occur **“within one or two business days,”** failure may cause “delay or denial,” applies call-in rules absolutely, categorically requires fitness certification, and says GSTC may recover premiums “under most circumstances.” FMLA regulations require practicability/context, eligibility/rights/designation notices, notice of fitness requirements, essential-functions lists when applicable, and recognize exceptions to premium recovery. The policy also omits some administration details and duplicates benefit paragraphs.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM/HIGH if used to deny leave:** 29 U.S.C. §§ 2615, 2617 and 29 C.F.R. Part 825.
- **Recommendation:** Replace with a current DOL-aligned policy, including employer notice duties, cure periods, intermittent increments, substitution rules, properly noticed fitness certifications, key-employee process, and limited premium recovery. Preserve more protective CFRA rules.

### 32. PFL/SDI descriptions are visibly outdated and contain incorrect waiting-period text

- **Location:** Paid Family Leave Insurance; Crime Victim Leave “Disability Insurance”; PDL (source lines 1910–1930, 2011–2019, 2098–2102).
- **Problem:** PFL says **“up to six eight weeks.”** SDI/PDL says an employee must be unable to work **“for at least eight days”** or PDL must last eight days, reflecting obsolete waiting-period concepts. Family/reason coverage should be updated for current EDD rules.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** Misrepresentation can deter benefit claims and create leave-administration errors.
- **Recommendation:** Use current EDD language or a short neutral referral stating PFL/DI are wage-replacement programs, not leave rights; remove benefit amounts/waiting periods likely to become stale; distinguish job protection under CFRA/FMLA/PDL/FEHA.

### 33. Expired 2020 FFCRA policies should not appear as current handbook provisions

- **Location:** Emergency Paid Sick Leave (COVID-19) and Expanded FMLA (COVID-19) (source lines 1550–1693).
- **Problem:** Roughly 140 lines describe benefits that expired December 31, 2020, in a “Revised 2026” handbook.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** Employees may claim handbook-promised benefits; administrators may apply obsolete rules; the stale content undermines the at-will/noncontract disclaimers and handbook reliability.
- **Recommendation:** Delete from the active handbook. Retain historical policies only in dated archives. Add a short statement that GSTC will comply with any currently effective federal, state, or local emergency leave mandate.

### 34. Progressive-discipline and performance promises create avoidable implied-contract arguments

- **Location:** Performance Evaluation; Disciplinary Process; Pay Raises (source lines 777–809).
- **Problem:** **“GSTC conducts”** a 90-day and annual review; performance **“will be reviewed in writing at least annually”;** management **“will first provide”** staged warnings and a supervisor **“will make every effort possible”** to allow a response. Disclaimers help, but mandatory promises conflict with later discretionary language.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** Implied-contract/promissory-estoppel and inconsistent-treatment evidence, particularly alongside “efforts will be made” to give raises.
- **Recommendation:** Remove duplicate mandatory formulations. State reviews/coaching may occur at GSTC’s discretion and do not alter at-will status; preserve the right to skip steps. Train managers not to promise guaranteed procedures.

### 35. At-will modification language is internally inconsistent

- **Location:** Foreword, At-Will Employment, Acknowledgment (source lines 82–166, 2525–2543).
- **Problem:** One passage says contracts require the President’s signature; another says changes to at-will status must be signed by **both President and employee**; the acknowledgment says no communication can modify the handbook. It also claims other undisclosed documents are “controlling,” potentially incorporating conflicting promises.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** Ambiguity supports implied-contract and authority disputes despite generally strong at-will language.
- **Recommendation:** Use one clause everywhere: only a specific written agreement signed by the President and employee can alter at-will status. State plan documents control only the applicable benefit plan and cannot alter at-will status. Remove absolute language inconsistent with written amendments.

### 36. Exempt classification and salary-deduction treatment are underdeveloped

- **Location:** Employment Categories; Holiday Pay; FMLA reduced schedule (source lines 490–517, 1360–1370, 1765–1769).
- **Problem:** The handbook merely says classified exempt employees are ineligible for overtime; holiday pay speaks in “8 hours” for exempt staff, which can imply hourly treatment. No salary-basis safe-harbor complaint policy appears, and classification depends on actual duties and California salary thresholds.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM/HIGH if classifications are wrong:** Overtime, meal/rest, wage statements, class/PAGA and FLSA collective claims.
- **Recommendation:** Audit every exempt role under California duties and current salary tests. Describe exempt employees as receiving a salary subject only to lawful deductions, avoid docking partial days, and add a salary-deduction complaint/reimbursement safe harbor without implying classification is determined by title.

### 37. Voting leave omits California’s paid entitlement and notice/posting details

- **Location:** Voting Leave (source lines 2187–2191).
- **Problem:** It promises only a **“reasonable time off”** and does not state that up to two hours are paid when the employee lacks sufficient nonworking time, normally at the beginning or end of shift, with advance notice where known.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM:** Elections Code §§ 14000–14001 wage and posting violations.
- **Recommendation:** State up to two paid hours when needed, timing rules, two-working-day notice where foreseeable, and posting obligations.

### 38. Workplace violence plan description may not satisfy access/training/record requirements

- **Location:** Workplace Violence Prevention and WVPP (source lines 397–411, 2518–2524).
- **Problem:** It says the plan is in IIPP binders and “integrated” into safety, but Labor Code § 6401.9 requires a compliant written plan, employee involvement, training, incident log, inspections, record retention, and access. A handbook summary does not establish compliance; “each worksite” and mobile crews require practical access.
- **Legal risk — MEDIUM/HIGH:** Cal/OSHA citation and inadequate response to violence incidents.
- **Recommendation:** Audit the actual standalone/integrated WVPP and violent-incident log against § 6401.9; ensure employee/representative access within required time, annual and event-triggered training, mobile-worksite procedures, and five-year incident-log retention.

## Arbitration and non-compete review

- **Location:** No arbitration clause or non-compete clause appears in the reviewed handbook.
- **Risk:** Their absence is not itself a defect. Do **not** insert either casually. California broadly voids employee non-competes (Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600 et seq.) and imposes civil consequences for presenting void restraints; confidentiality/non-solicitation language must not operate as a de facto non-compete. Any arbitration agreement should be separate, voluntary in presentation, supported by contract formation, mutual, procedurally fair, and drafted for current FAA/California law. PAGA waivers and representative-action treatment require specialist review because the law continues to develop.
- **Recommendation:** Have counsel review all onboarding, separation, confidentiality, customer/employee nonsolicitation, repayment, and arbitration documents outside this handbook. Do not make handbook acknowledgment assent to arbitration.

## PAGA/class-action exposure map

The most likely representative or aggregate theories are: unpaid pre/post-shift and travel time; meal/rest premiums; regular-rate errors for emergency incentives and field allowances; business-expense reimbursement; unlawful deductions; sick-leave denial/rehire errors; inaccurate wage statements; and final-pay waiting-time penalties. These are operational risks, not cured by better words alone.

Before rollout, counsel should direct a privileged audit using time punches, GPS/dispatch records, yard departure/return times, meal edits, premium codes, incentive calculations, phone/tool/mileage reimbursements, uniform/equipment deductions, leave denials, and final checks for at least the legally relevant lookback periods. Corrective payments and policy changes should be planned together.

8. Train HR, payroll, dispatchers, supervisors, and crew leaders before obtaining fresh acknowledgments.
